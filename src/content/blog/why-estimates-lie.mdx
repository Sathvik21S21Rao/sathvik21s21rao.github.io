"How long will it take?" sounds like a simple question. It has the grammar of a simple question, the same shape as "how long is this table" or "how far is the airport." Those questions have answers you can get with a tape measure or a map. Software estimates don't work that way, and the mismatch between how the question sounds and how it actually behaves is the entire reason engineers are notorious for getting it wrong.

## The two-week myth

There's a running joke that every task, no matter its actual size, gets estimated at "two weeks." The joke is funny because it's close enough to true to sting. But the punchline isn't that engineers are bad at their jobs; it's that the estimate is being asked to do something it structurally can't do: predict the size of a problem you haven't solved yet.

Here's the asymmetry that breaks the whole exercise. Think about a three-day bug with a one-line fix. Before you find a bug, you don't know if it's a five-minute typo or a three-day hunt through a race condition, and you don't know because *knowing* would require having already solved it. Estimating a task and doing the task both require understanding the task, and you only get that understanding by doing the work.

## Where the time actually goes

Ask an engineer to build a login page and they'll picture: a form, a database check, a success message. That's the part that's easy to estimate, and it's usually a small fraction of the actual work. The rest tends to be things nobody puts a number on because nobody thinks to ask about them upfront:

- What happens when the password is wrong three times in a row?
- What happens if the database is slow, or briefly unreachable?
- Does this need to work for someone using a screen reader?
- What happens to a user who was logged in when their account got deleted mid-session?

None of that is scope creep in the usual, annoyed sense of the word. It's not new work sneaking in from outside; it was always part of "build a login page." It just wasn't visible from the outside, the way a house's foundation isn't visible from the street.

### Unknown unknowns, not just unknowns

There's a useful distinction between two kinds of uncertainty. A *known unknown* is a question you know you need to answer, like "which payment provider are we using," and you can put a number on finding out. An *unknown unknown* is a question you don't yet know exists, and by definition you cannot schedule time for a question you haven't thought to ask. Almost every estimate blows up because of the second kind, not the first.

> Hofstadter's Law: it always takes longer than you expect, even when you take Hofstadter's Law into account.

That's not a cute paradox, it's a description of a system that doesn't converge. Padding an estimate helps a little. Padding it because *last time* it took longer than expected helps a little more. But the underlying problem (that you can't fully see a task's shape until you're inside it) doesn't go away no matter how experienced you get. Experience mostly helps you guess which category of surprise is coming, not avoid it.

## A small, honest formula

Engineers sometimes only half-jokingly use something like this:

```text
real_time = your_estimate × 2, one unit size larger
# "three days" becomes "six weeks"
# "an afternoon" becomes "two days"
```

It's a joke with teeth, because it captures something the tape-measure model of estimation can't: the error isn't random noise scattered evenly around the right answer. It's a systematic bias in one direction, because there are vastly more ways for a task to be secretly bigger than you think than there are for it to be secretly smaller. Nobody ever discovers a login page didn't need a database after all.

## What actually helps

Not a fix, because there isn't one, but a few things that shrink the gap:

1. **Estimate ranges, not points.** "Three to ten days" is more honest, and more useful, than a confident "five."
2. **Break work down until each piece is small enough that you've basically already thought through how to build it.** Uncertainty hides in the pieces you haven't looked at closely.
3. **Track how wrong you were, and in which direction**, the same way you'd log a bug's symptoms before hunting the cause: you can't fix a bias you haven't measured.
4. **Say "I don't know yet" out loud** when the honest answer is that the unknowns haven't been found. It's a better answer than a wrong number, even if it's a less comfortable one to give.

The tape measure was never the right tool. A better mental model is closer to weather forecasting: a range, a confidence level, and an honest admission that the forecast gets worse the further out you look.
