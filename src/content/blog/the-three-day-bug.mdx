Picture the scenario, because some version of it happens to every engineer sooner or later. A report comes in: something is broken, but only sometimes. Not on your machine. Not in the obvious cases. Just sometimes, in a way nobody can reliably reproduce.

You spend the first hour confirming the bug is even real, which sounds like it should take five minutes and never does. You have to rule out "user error," rule out "already fixed in the last deploy," rule out "this is secretly two different bugs wearing one trench coat." By the end of day one you at least know the shape of the problem, even if you have no idea what's causing it.

## The usual suspects

Every engineer keeps a mental lineup of culprits, and you work through it in order:

- A bad deploy: something changed recently and broke quietly.
- Bad data: some input nobody anticipated, sitting in the database like a landmine.
- A dependency update: a library changed behavior out from under you.
- Timing: the bug only shows up under load, or only on a slow connection, or only when two things happen in almost the same instant.

The first three are satisfying to rule out because they're checkable. You can diff the deploy, query the data, pin the dependency version. Timing bugs are worse, because "checking" mostly means staring at logs and hoping the failure repeats itself in front of you instead of behind your back.

This one turned out to be timing. Specifically, it was a <Term def="When two things try to happen at the same time, in an order the programmer didn't plan for, and the result depends on which one finishes first.">race condition</Term>: two parts of the system both reaching for the same piece of data, and the bug only appeared when they reached for it in the wrong order. Most of the time they didn't. Occasionally they did.

(If you want the mechanical reason `await` was the actual fix here rather than just the incantation that happened to work, there's a [[await-and-the-event-loop|short piece on what the event loop is actually promising you]].)

## Why this takes three days

Here's the uncomfortable truth about bugs like this: the difficulty isn't in writing the fix. It's in getting the bug to hold still long enough to look at. You can't fix what you can't reliably see, and a race condition by definition doesn't want to be seen; it only shows up when the timing is unlucky, which is exactly the condition you can't force on demand.

So most of day two is spent adding logging, narrowing down *where* the wrong order happens, and building a way to reproduce the failure on purpose instead of waiting for it to happen by accident. That reproduction step is the real work. Once you can make a bug happen reliably, you've basically already solved it; you just haven't written the fix yet.

> An old debugging saying holds up better than most: the bug is never where you think it is. It's always one door down.

By the middle of day three, the reproduction was solid, and the cause was obvious in hindsight, obvious in the way these things always are once you can finally see them. One piece of code assumed a value was already set. Another piece of code, running a beat earlier under the wrong conditions, hadn't set it yet.

## The fix

```js
// Before: assumes `cache.ready` is already true
function getUser(id) {
  return cache.users[id]; // sometimes undefined, sometimes not
}

// After: wait for the thing that has to happen first, to actually happen first
async function getUser(id) {
  await cache.ready;
  return cache.users[id];
}
```

One line, functionally. An `await` in front of a promise that should have been there from the start. Three days of searching, five seconds of typing.

That gap (three days to find, five seconds to fix) is not a failure of skill. It's the actual shape of the job. Estimating how long a bug will take is a special case of a problem covered in [[why-estimates-lie|a different kind of estimating problem]]: you cannot know how long a search will take until you've already found what you were looking for. The size of the eventual fix tells you nothing in advance about the size of the search required to find it.

## What actually helped

Nothing clever, in the end. A short list of things that made the three days shorter than they could have been:

1. Writing down what you've already ruled out, so you don't check it twice out of panic.
2. Building a reliable reproduction before trying to build a fix.
3. Being suspicious of anything involving order, timing, or "this only happens sometimes": that's almost always a race condition in disguise.
4. Accepting that the search and the fix are two different problems with two different sizes.

The same instinct (that something behaves fine until you put real conditions on it) shows up outside of debugging too, in places like [[what-the-cloud-actually-is|distributed systems that are quietly held together by timing assumptions]]. Once you've hunted one race condition, you start noticing the shape of them everywhere.
